roniam.dev = Ron + IAM · identity & access management

Hi, I'm Ron. I AM what I do.

When my kids were little, both of them insisted I read Green Eggs and Ham cover to cover, every single night. Sam-I-Am never left me. So here is mine: Ron-I-AM. Identity in both senses, the field I work in and who I am.

I'm Ron Bar-Tor, a Senior Engineering Manager. My corner of engineering is identity, the load-bearing machinery that decides who you are and what you may touch. I've spent years in the OAuth dance (2.0 day to day, a smidge of 2.1 where it matters), in SAML's enterprise plumbing, in passkeys and Kerberos tickets, and in the thousand ways login can ruin a Tuesday. Machine learning is the other love, found in graduate school, where regression sounded like going backwards, PCA sounded like an accounting certification, and the confusion and headaches followed quickly; ML Dojo is my answer, plain English first, math second. I built this site to pass on what I've accumulated: five learning paths spanning identity, software engineering, JavaScript, ML, and cloud-native infrastructure from Docker to Istio, plus an engineering blog. I use AI the way a leader uses a team, delegating the mechanical and keeping the judgment (more on that just below). The aim is to develop engineers, not park them in tutorial hell. Every lesson ends with you doing the work, and CI gates hold the material to the same bar I'd hold a team to.

Where AI fits, and where it doesn't

I'll be straightforward: AI generated this site, and it generated large parts of the dojos. That was a deliberate engineering decision, and it's the same call I make as a technical leader every day: know what to delegate, and know what only you can do. The curriculum, the architecture, the design decisions, and every technical judgment in these lessons are mine, drawn from years of hands-on work in identity and platform engineering. When the problem needed my hands, it got them: the grading model, the CI gates, the protocol details a generator would happily get wrong. When the work was mechanical, I delegated it and reviewed the output, exactly as I would with a team. The typing was delegated. The thinking wasn't. And the same five CI gates hold every line to the bar, no matter who or what wrote it.

Proof of work

Don't take my word for it. Take the course.

Tutorial hell is watching someone else code. The way out is doing the work, so every lesson here ends with an exercise you complete yourself, in the page. Five learning paths; the three core dojos compile to a single self-contained HTML file with zero dependencies, so they run offline, from a USB stick if you like, while ML Dojo lives in its own repository and the cloud-native path takes you from Docker to a running Istio mesh. Every push is gated by five CI checks, including one that executes every graded exercise against its own reference solution. Free, no accounts, no tracking. Knowledge shared is the whole point.

The IAM in roniam

Where authentication meets production.

I've spent years in the identity domain, and I've done it at scale. I've seen tokens that expire at the worst possible time, redirects pointing to the wrong place, federation between companies that don't really know how that works, SSO as a protocol rather than an experience, and authentication code that worked until it didn't. If you want to know how I actually think about this stuff, take the stream. Every flow is documented and diagrammed. The Identity Dojo is designed with plain English first and technicalities later, and I also dig into the attacks that necessitated these solutions. Start wherever it hurts: OAuth 2.0 & OIDC · SAML & Web SSO · WebAuthn & passkeys · Kerberos & AD · service-to-service auth & SPIFFE · advanced OAuth threats · running identity.

Management, practiced

How I lead

This is what I believe and live. This is what I say and do with my teams.

“Build trust, then unleash.”

I can't do everything, and pretending otherwise helps nobody. So delegation starts with building trust, and then I unleash the engineers I work with on the problems themselves. That's how I get results, and it's how engineers grow, feel empowered, and love the work.

“Honest, direct, in every direction.”

Between me and my engineers, my managers, my peers, and the rest of the organization, communication has to be honest and direct, tuned to what works best for each person. When a call to action is needed, I call to action. I celebrate wins and I own failures. And when a problem arises, I don't just highlight the problem, I offer solutions.

“People first, engineers second.”

I get to know my people and learn what makes them tick. That's how they grow, and how I let them be their best. I value their opinions, and I'm willing to change direction when someone passionately believes in something, as long as they can prove it with data.

“Don't let perfect be the enemy of good.”

Sometimes it's OK, and smart, to do something later. Deferring on purpose is a decision. Over and over again I've seen that trying to "boil the ocean" is a recipe for analysis paralysis, overcomplication, and failure to launch.

“I make smart bets. There are no guarantees.”

Built on data and experience, and it's hard to argue with the data. Most decisions won't sink us, so we commit to what the data tells us. Could it go wrong? Sure. But I don't walk through one-way doors.

“Never stop learning.”

Being the smartest in the room rings my alarms. Not because being smart is bad, but because it means the learning has stopped. The best version of my job is being surrounded by people who regularly teach me something, and the dojos exist because teaching is how I keep forcing myself to learn.

“I deliver results, and I love impact.”

I love taking on new challenges, tackling hard problems, and solving real pain points. I work with the whole organization to deliver real results that exceed customer expectations, on time, and grounded in reality.
